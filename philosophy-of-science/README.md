# Abstract

At the end of the nineteenth century, in what might be called a positivist dream, Mach envisioned a future science emancipated from causation, regarded as a “formally obscure” by-product of earlier theories. There is a subtle irony in the way newer causation-sceptical theories defend themselves against concepts bearing the label “causal” in special relativity by appealing to their merely formal significance (Norton 2005, 2009; Smith 2013).

The Machian (and later Russellian) tradition has persisted through the revolutions that transformed the scientific landscape of the twentieth century. An intriguing continuation and original reappraisal of this tradition is offered by Norton in his 2003 essay “Causation as Folk Science.”

Norton introduces his *causal antifundamentalism* through two arguments. The negative argument seeks to eliminate the concept of causation from the fundamental ontology attributed to our best scientific theories, while the positive argument, in keeping with a Lakatosian methodological principle, provides a new framework capable of explaining the history of science.

The reception of Norton’s essay has focused primarily on the negative argument, and particularly on the claim that our mature sciences contain no law of causation that imposes constraints on the reality they describe. The literature contains numerous counterexamples to this claim, and I do not intend to take a position on the debates surrounding them. Instead, this paper argues that even if the objections advanced by way of counterexamples are assumed to be unsuccessful, the argument itself still fails.

To this end, I adopt the argumentative strategy introduced by P. D. Magnus and Craig Callender (2004). I argue that Norton’s negative argument can be paraphrased as a pessimistic induction and, in this form, commits the *base rate fallacy*. I first attempt to provide a formally adequate reconstruction of the argument that preserves its essential content, and then demonstrate its flaw through probabilistic analysis.

In the concluding section, I examine the notion of “folk” sciences introduced in Norton’s positive argument in light of the analogy previously established with the pessimistic meta-induction.
